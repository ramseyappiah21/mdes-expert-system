Abstract

This report describes the design, implementation, testing, and evaluation of CAAES (CSE Academic Advising Expert System), a knowledge-based expert system written in SWI-Prolog. The system advises undergraduate Computer Engineering students on course eligibility, semester planning, academic standing, internship and Final Year Project readiness, career-track alignment, and graduation. Domain knowledge is stored as first-order facts (courses, prerequisites, offerings, policy thresholds, and sample student records). Expert judgement is encoded as more than forty named Horn-clause rules. The inference layer demonstrates three classical reasoning styles required by the brief: backward (goal-driven) queries such as eligibility and graduation, forward (data-driven) derivation of standing and support actions, and recursive traversal of prerequisite hierarchies. A consultation module asks context-dependent questions, validates every answer, and prints an explanation for each recommendation. Automated tests confirm that the knowledge base exceeds eighty facts and forty rules and that representative decisions are stable.

Keywords: expert systems; Prolog; academic advising; backward chaining; forward chaining; recursive inference; knowledge explanation.

1. Introduction and problem definition

1.1 Problem statement

Undergraduate engineering programmes publish a dense web of constraints: prerequisites, semester offerings, credit-load limits, class-of-degree bands, and milestone gates such as internship and the Final Year Project. Students experience these rules as a sequence of local questions (“May I take Algorithms this semester?”) while advisors experience them as a global planning problem (“Given this transcript and this CGPA, what is the safest next step?”). Both views are logical. Both are easy to get wrong when the reasoning is done only in conversation.

The problem addressed by this project is therefore:

How can a knowledge-based system encode the regulations and the heuristic judgement of a CSE academic advisor so that a student can obtain an explained, consistent recommendation from incomplete or noisy input?

1.2 Why an expert system

Expert systems were among the earliest successful applications of artificial intelligence precisely because some professional tasks are already written as facts plus if–then rules. Academic advising is one of those tasks. The regulations are explicit; the heuristics (prefer cores, repair weak prerequisites, reduce load on warning) are stable enough to write down; and the user needs a reason, not a black-box rank. A statistical recommender could guess popular course combinations, but it could not prove eligibility or point at the missing ancestor on a prerequisite chain.

1.3 Aim and objectives

The aim is to design, implement, test, and evaluate a fully functional Prolog-based expert system for CSE academic advising.

  • Represent a self-contained Computer Engineering curriculum as Prolog facts.
  • Encode at least forty expert rules for eligibility, standing, load, milestones, and scoring.
  • Demonstrate backward, forward, and recursive inference on the same knowledge base.
  • Interview a user with questions that change according to year, CGPA, and interest.
  • Explain every recommendation and every refusal.
  • Recover from unknown, invalid, and missing input.
  • Document architecture, tests, and limits at a length suitable for academic submission.

2. Background: expert systems and Prolog

2.1 Expert systems

An expert system separates what is known from how it is applied. The classical architecture has three pieces: a knowledge base of facts and rules, an inference engine that applies those rules, and a working memory of case-specific data (here, the student’s transcript and answers). Optional facilities include an explanation module and a consultation (interview) layer. CAAES follows that architecture exactly, which is why the source tree is split into knowledge_base.pl, rules.pl, inference.pl, explanation.pl, validation.pl, and consultation.pl.

Two control strategies dominate textbooks. Backward chaining starts from a goal and asks what would have to be true. It matches medical diagnosis (“Is this malaria?”) and eligibility (“Is this student allowed CE301?”). Forward chaining starts from known facts and fires every rule whose conditions are satisfied, pushing new conclusions into working memory. It matches monitoring and “tell me everything that follows from this transcript.” CAAES implements both, plus recursion for hierarchical structure.

2.2 Prolog as an implementation language

Prolog (Programming in Logic) is a natural host for this architecture. A fact such as prerequisite(ce301, ce201). is already a logical atom. A rule such as 

eligible_for(Student, Course) :-
    course(Course, _, _, _),
    \+ has_passed(Student, Course),
    all_prerequisites_satisfied(Student, Course).

is already a Horn clause. SLD resolution performs backward chaining for free: when the user asks eligible_for(ama, ce301), the engine searches for a proof. That is why the assignment specifies SWI-Prolog rather than a general-purpose language in which the same search would have to be written by hand.

SWI-Prolog additionally provides dynamic predicates (so an interview can assertz a guest transcript), forall/2 for universal checks, and libraries for line-oriented input. CAAES uses those facilities but does not depend on rare extensions; the program is ordinary Edinburgh-style Prolog.

2.3 Related advising systems

Course-advising expert systems appear throughout the AI-education literature, often as MYCIN-style interviews over prerequisite tables. CAAES is deliberately in that tradition, with three additions that raise it above a toy lookup: (1) a scored planner that packs a semester to a standing-aware credit limit; (2) a recursive closure over the prerequisite graph, used both to block illegal registration and to explain the shortest missing path; (3) milestone rules (internship, FYP, honours, graduation) that sit on top of the same facts. The curriculum codes are original to this project; they are inspired by a typical four-year CSE programme and include CE474, Logic of Computer Science, as a named core.

3. Domain justification and scope

3.1 Target user and decisions

Checkpoint (brief): the target user is an undergraduate Computer Engineering / Computer Science student, or a faculty advisor acting for that student. The expert system helps them decide (i) which courses they may legally take next semester, (ii) how many credits are safe given standing, (iii) whether internship, Final Year Project, or graduation gates are open, and (iv) which electives and career track fit declared interests.

The domain was chosen over medical diagnosis or vehicle faults for three reasons. First, the assignment’s own illustrative facts are already student/course/eligibility triples; building on that vocabulary keeps the report aligned with the lecture examples. Second, prerequisite graphs are a textbook case of recursive reasoning, which medical symptom lists often lack. Third, every group member is already a domain informant: we know what a bad semester plan looks like.

3.2 In scope

  • A 43-course catalogue across levels 100–400, with offerings and departments.
  • Direct prerequisites and their transitive closure.
  • Core versus elective classification and a minimum elective count.
  • CGPA bands used in Ghanaian university class-of-degree practice.
  • Eight sample students covering success, recovery, and near-graduation.
  • A guest interview that builds a temporary student record.

3.3 Out of scope

  • Official UMaT Senate regulations and live student-information-system data.
  • Timetable clashes, room capacity, and lecturer availability.
  • Fee status, visa status, or disciplinary holds.
  • Natural-language understanding beyond tokenised yes/no and course codes.
  • Machine-learned ranking of electives from historical enrolment.

These exclusions are intentional. An expert system that pretends to have authority it does not have is more dangerous than one that states its limits. The user manual repeats that CAAES is reasoned advice over a teaching knowledge base, not a registrar’s decision.

4. System architecture

4.1 Logical view

Practice (brief): user input, the inference engine, and the knowledge base interact as a closed loop. The consultation layer never writes recommendations itself. It only asserts session facts and then asks the engine the same questions a programmer would type at the Prolog prompt.

Working memory for a guest interview is a small set of dynamic predicates: student(guest), passed(guest, Course), declared_gpa(guest, GPA), student_year(guest, Year), student_interest(guest, Interest), and session flags such as weak_programming. Sample students are ordinary static facts; the same rules apply to both.

5. Knowledge representation

5.1 Design approach

Knowledge is represented as ground Prolog facts. There is no external database and no XML rule file. That choice keeps every demonstration inside one language: the examiner can open the knowledge base, edit a fact, reload, and watch a recommendation change. Predicates are given stable arities and English names so that the rule layer reads like a specification.

5.2 Principal fact types

Table 4. Knowledge-base predicates (selected)

A course is identified by a lowercase atom (ce301) and displayed in uppercase. Levels are 100, 200, 300, and 400. Credits are integers. Letter grades map through grade_point/2 using a four-point scale (A = 4.00 … F = 0.00).

5.3 Prerequisite graph

The prerequisite relation is a directed acyclic graph. There is no course that requires itself, directly or indirectly. That invariant matters: recursive rules in Chapter 7 would not terminate on a cycle. The longest teaching example is the path from CE407 (Final Year Project II) back to CE101 (Introduction to Computing), which walks CE406, CE310, CE207, CE202, CE104, and CE102. 

prerequisite(ce102, ce101).
prerequisite(ce104, ce102).
prerequisite(ce201, ce104).
prerequisite(ce301, ce201).
% ...
prerequisite(ce406, ce310).
prerequisite(ce407, ce406).

5.4 Policy facts

Numeric policy is data, not hard-wired arithmetic inside every rule. Changing the first-class band or the graduation credit minimum is a one-line edit: 

standing_threshold(first_class, 3.60).
standing_threshold(warning, 1.50).
graduation_credit_minimum(120).
internship_credit_minimum(54).
min_electives_required(4).
recommended_credits(probation, 12).

This is the knowledge-engineer view of an expert system: the programmer writes the shape of the rule; the knowledge engineer can still retune thresholds without touching control flow.

5.6 Volume

The assignment asks for at least eighty facts. CAAES stores 529 unit clauses: 43 courses, 43 offerings, 43 department links, a dense prerequisite list, cores and electives, interest and skill tags, policy rows, and full transcripts for the sample students. The test suite counts these clauses at runtime and fails if the total drops below eighty. That count is a guard, not a target; the extra facts exist because a believable curriculum is larger than a toy.

6. Rule-base description

6.1 Design principles

Rules are written as named predicates with comments R01–R50. A rule has a single responsibility. Eligibility does not also print text. Scoring does not also decide graduation. Explanation predicates in a separate file read the same rules; they do not duplicate the logic in English.

Negation is used only as negation-as-failure over a closed catalogue (\+ has_passed(Student, Course)). That is acceptable because the universe of courses is finite and listed. We do not treat “not in the transcript” as a medical unknown; we treat it as “not yet passed.”

6.3 Eligibility (core expert rule)

eligible_for(Student, Course) :-
    course(Course, _, _, CourseLevel),
    \+ has_passed(Student, Course),
    all_prerequisites_satisfied(Student, Course),
    progression_level(Student, StudentLevel),
    (   at_risk(Student)
    ->  CourseLevel =< StudentLevel
    ;   CourseLevel =< StudentLevel + 100
    ).

all_prerequisites_satisfied(Student, Course) :-
    forall(prerequisite(Course, Required),
           has_passed(Student, Required)).

A student may take a catalogue course that they have not already passed, once every direct prerequisite is on the transcript and the course is not more than one level above their year (or not above their year if they are at risk). That last guard stops a student on warning from being handed a 400-level core that happens to have no prerequisites. Indirect ancestors are enforced automatically: if CE201 is missing, CE301 is missing a direct prerequisite, so the universal check fails. The recursive ancestor predicate is therefore not required for legality; it is required for explanation and for “how far back is the blockage?”

6.4 Academic standing

Standing is a first-match cascade from first class down to probation. Thresholds live in the knowledge base. gpa/2 prefers a declared CGPA (used for Kofi and for guest interviews) and otherwise averages recorded letter grades. at_risk/1 is true for warning or probation and drives the reduced-load rule.

6.5 Priority scoring

Legal eligibility is not the same as a good plan. A year-3 student might be eligible for a low-value elective and for a gating core in the same semester. course_priority_score/3 adds points for core status (+5), interest match (+3), gating value (+2 if the course unlocks two or more later courses), catch-up of a lower level (+4), and level fit (+2 or +3). The semester planner sorts by this score and then packs courses until the standing-aware credit limit is reached (18 / 15 / 12, never above 21).

6.6 Milestone rules

Internship readiness requires year ≥ 3, credits at or above the internship minimum, CE207 and CE204 passed, and CGPA ≥ 2.50. Final Year Project I requires year ≥ 4, CE310, and a higher credit floor. Graduation is conjunctive: credit minimum, zero remaining cores, enough electives, both FYP courses, and CGPA ≥ 2.00. Near-graduation is a weaker predicate used for advice when the student is in year 4 with few remaining cores but is not yet fully eligible.

6.7 Completeness

Appendix B lists all fifty named rules. The test suite fails if that list shrinks below forty. Several rules have more than one clause (academic_standing/2, support_action/2, next_milestone/2); the assignment’s “forty rules” is interpreted as forty distinct expert predicates, which is the unit a knowledge engineer would count on a rule sheet.

7. Inference: backward, forward, and recursive reasoning

7.1 Backward reasoning (goal-driven)

Checkpoint (brief): backward reasoning is more natural than forward reasoning when the user already has a concrete yes/no question. “Is Ama eligible for CE301?” is such a question. The engine starts from eligible_for(ama, ce301) and works back to course/4, \+ passed(ama, ce301), and passed(ama, ce201). It does not need to derive Kofi’s standing or Akosua’s graduation status on the way. Menu option 3 exposes this mode. Graduation and internship questions are the same pattern with different goals.

Prolog’s default search is backward chaining. CAAES does not re-implement a resolver; it poses goals and lets SLD resolution do the work. The wrapper predicates in inference.pl only normalise the answer to yes/no for documentation and tests.

7.2 Forward reasoning (data-driven)

Forward reasoning starts from everything already known about one student and asks what else must be true. run_forward_steps/2 repeatedly looks for a conclusion that is not yet in working memory — standing, each unlocked course, internship readiness, graduation, support actions — and asserts it as derived/2. The loop stops when a full pass adds nothing. A second predicate, forward_infer/1, records an aggregated profile (credit total, GPA, remaining cores, primary track) for the printed demo.

This is data-driven in the textbook sense: Kofi never types “am I at risk?” The facts declared_gpa(kofi, 1.70) and the standing thresholds produce standing(warning) and then support(reduce_load). Menu option 5 is the live demonstration.

7.3 Recursive reasoning

Hierarchical structure appears as the prerequisite graph. Two mutually illustrative predicates are: 

ancestor_prerequisite(Course, Required) :-
    prerequisite(Course, Required).
ancestor_prerequisite(Course, Required) :-
    prerequisite(Course, Mid),
    ancestor_prerequisite(Mid, Required).

prerequisite_path(Course, Required, [Course, Required]) :-
    prerequisite(Course, Required).
prerequisite_path(Course, Required, [Course|Path]) :-
    prerequisite(Course, Mid),
    prerequisite_path(Mid, Required, Path).

The first is a classic transitive closure. The second retains the path so the explanation facility can print the shortest blocked chain. Depth and “longest path” predicates support the catalogue explorer (menu 4). Tests require that ancestor_prerequisite(ce407, ce101) succeeds and that CE310 is an ancestor but not a direct prerequisite of CE407.

7.4 What-if as a controlled forward step

what_if_unlocks/3 asks which courses would become eligible if one extra course were marked passed, without asserting that fact into the database. It is a hypothetical forward step used in planning conversations (“If I take CE201 now, what opens next year?”). For Kwame, passing CE201 newly unlocks CE301 and CE303.

7.5 Closed-world assumption

All three modes assume a closed world for the catalogue and an open world for the guest transcript. If CE399 is not a fact, it is not a course. If a guest does not list CE104, they have not passed CE104. The interview can add facts; it cannot add courses. That distinction is what keeps unknown input from inventing a parallel curriculum.

8. User interaction

8.1 Modes

The main menu offers an interview, a sample-student report, a backward eligibility query, a recursive chain explorer, a forward-inference demo, a what-if query, a catalogue, self-checks, and exit. This is more than a single dialog, because the brief asks the group to demonstrate reasoning styles, not only to hide them behind one recommendation.

8.2 Context-dependent questions

The interview is not a fixed questionnaire. After year and CGPA are known, later prompts branch:

  • Year 1 asks for Level 100 courses only and never mentions FYP.
  • Year ≥ 2 asks whether CE104 is passed.
  • Year ≥ 3 asks for a second interest, CE207, and internship intention. An AI interest additionally asks about MA202 comfort.
  • Year 4 asks about CE310 and CE406.
  • CGPA < 2.00 asks about failed programming courses and stores a recovery flag.

That branching is the “intelligent, context-dependent questions” requirement. It is implemented as ordinary Prolog conditions around ask_yes_no/2, not as a separate planning language.

8.3 Report

The advising report always contains: profile (year, semester, focus, CGPA, credits, interests); standing explanation; next milestone; support actions; a semester plan of course cards; career-track and industry suggestions; and a graduation snapshot. Focus itself is a rule (consultation_focus/2): recovery, foundations, specialisation, or graduation.

8.4 Comparison with the brief’s medical dialog

The assignment’s example asks “Do you have fever?” then “Do you have headache?” and prints a diagnosis. CAAES asks “What is your year?” then only those clinical questions that still matter, and prints a plan plus reasons. The control pattern is the same: interview → infer → explain. The vocabulary is academic rather than medical because that is the chosen domain.

9. Knowledge explanation

9.1 Design (checkpoint)

Checkpoint (brief): explanation information is collected as the list of satisfied conditions for the decision that was just taken, then displayed as bullet text. CAAES does not store a parallel “explanation knowledge base.” It re-queries the same rules that produced the answer and renders each success as a sentence.

  • explain_course/3 — why a course is recommended (prerequisites, core, catch-up, interest, gating, level).
  • explain_ineligible/3 — why a course is refused (already passed, missing direct prerequisite, blocked ancestor path).
  • explain_standing/2 — GPA, class-of-degree phrase, warning reasons.
  • explain_graduation/2 — credits, cores, electives, FYP, verdict.

9.2 Example: positive explanation

For Ama and CE301 the collected reasons include: all listed prerequisites are passed; CE301 is a core; the course is at level 300; and passing it unlocks later work (CE305 among others). The printed card also shows the numeric priority score so a sceptical advisor can see that the ranking is not arbitrary.

9.3 Example: negative explanation

For Yaw and CE301 the system reports that CE201 is a missing direct prerequisite and that earlier ancestors (CE104, CE102, CE101 is already passed) still sit on the chain. The shortest blocked path is printed so the student sees a repair sequence rather than a single “no.”

9.4 Honesty

If no extra reason fires, the printer says so rather than inventing a paragraph. Empty reason lists are treated as a defect in tests for the standard demo cases, where at least one reason must exist.

11. Implementation notes

11.1 Language and libraries

The implementation is SWI-Prolog using library(lists) and library(readutil). There are no foreign-language wrappers. Dynamic predicates that the interview may extend are declared at the top of the knowledge base so assertz/1 is legal.

11.2 Control decisions

Cuts (!) appear only where a classification must not backtrack into a worse band (GPA standing, declared-GPA preference, consultation focus). They are not used to “make the program faster” in the planner. Failure-driven loops appear in the forward-step engine and in forall checks, which is idiomatic Prolog rather than an accident.

11.3 Display versus identity

Internal atoms stay lowercase. All user-facing printers call upcase_course/2. Input is lower-cased before matching. That prevents the classic student bug in which CE301 and ce301 become two different courses.

11.4 How to run

From the project root: run.bat or swipl -s src/main.pl. Tests: test.bat or swipl -q -s tests/test_suite.pl. Programmers can consult('src/loader.pl') and type the queries in Chapter 12 without starting the menu.

13. Testing strategy and results

13.1 Strategy

Testing has three layers. Automated predicates in tests/test_suite.pl act as regression oracles for the eight sample students and for validation helpers. Menu self-checks (option 8) repeat a subset of those oracles for the live demo. Manual scripts cover interview branching and invalid input, which are awkward to drive without a pseudo-terminal.

The strategy is specification-based, not random. Each automated case is a sentence from Chapters 3, 6, or 7 (“Yaw must not be eligible for CE301”). When a rule changes, the suite is the first place a contradiction appears.

14. Evaluation: strengths and limitations

14.1 Strengths

  • One knowledge base, three inference styles. The same facts answer a goal, push conclusions, and walk a hierarchy. That is the pedagogical point of the course.
  • Explanations are derived, not canned. Changing a prerequisite changes both the decision and the why-not text.
  • Standing-aware planning. Kofi cannot receive Ama’s credit load by accident; the load rule is tied to at_risk/1.
  • Closed catalogue, open transcript. Invalid courses cannot sneak in; missing student data is handled as unknown or default.
  • Demo-ready oracles. Eight students cover the stories an examiner is likely to ask for.
  • Volume with structure. Hundreds of facts are grouped by predicate, not dumped as an unreadable wall.

14.2 Limitations

  • Teaching curriculum, not the official handbook. Course codes and credit totals are internally consistent but are not a registrar extract. Using CAAES for a real registration would be misuse.
  • No clash detection. Two recommended courses might share a real timetable slot. The knowledge base has no timetable facts.
  • CGPA is a simple average of recorded grades (or a declared number). Credit-weighted GPA and repeated-course replacement rules are not modelled.
  • Interest matching is keyword overlap, not a psychological instrument. Two shared tags beat one; there is no decay or recency.
  • Forward chaining is student-scoped, not a general RETE network. That is sufficient for the brief and easier to explain in a ten-minute demo.
  • Interview branching is coded, not driven by a separate question-selection rule engine. Adding a new year-5 programme would require new clauses in consultation.pl.
  • Single-user, single-session. There is no login, no saved guest file, and no concurrency.

14.3 Ethical note

An advising system can harm a student if it is over-trusted. CAAES therefore prints evidence (GPA, credits, missing cores) beside every verdict and states in the user manual that it does not replace faculty advice. Defaults for unknown CGPA are conservative. The system will not invent a passed course to make a plan look full.

15. Proposed future improvements

  1. Official curriculum import. Load the real departmental handbook as facts, with an audit trail of the version date.
  2. Credit-weighted GPA and repeat-and-replace rules as used by the examinations office.
  3. Timetable and clash facts so a semester plan is physically attendable.
  4. Question-selection rules that pick the next interview question by information gain (still symbolic, still explainable).
  5. Persistent guest records in a Prolog save file, with explicit consent text.
  6. Graphical prerequisite viewer that draws the path currently printed as a list.
  7. Bilingual prompts (English / Twi) without changing the rule layer — only the validation lexicon.
  8. Advisor override facts (waiver(Student, Course)) so a documented exemption participates in eligibility.
  9. Broader regression pack that drives the interview through a recorded answer file.
  10. Formal proof notes exported beside each report for the student’s file.
